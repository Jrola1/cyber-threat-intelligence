# Confidence Language

Confidence statements communicate the strength of the evidential and analytical basis for a judgement. They do not represent mathematical probabilities.

## High Confidence

The judgement is supported by strong, credible and generally corroborated evidence. Important intelligence gaps may remain, but they are unlikely to overturn the central assessment.

**High confidence does not mean certainty.**

## Moderate Confidence

The judgement is supported by credible evidence and a coherent analytical basis, but relevant gaps, source limitations or plausible alternatives remain. Additional information could materially refine or alter the assessment.

## Low Confidence

The judgement is based on limited, incomplete, indirect or weakly corroborated evidence. The assessment may be analytically plausible but significant alternatives or intelligence gaps remain.

## Hypotheses

A hypothesis should not be upgraded into an established fact simply because it is technically plausible. Where appropriate, hypotheses include:

- supporting evidence;
- assumptions;
- alternative explanations;
- confidence;
- intelligence gaps; and
- evidence required to confirm or reject the hypothesis.

## Language

Assessments may use estimative language such as **likely**, **probably**, **possibly**, or **unlikely** where useful, but these terms should be accompanied by enough reasoning and confidence context for the reader to understand why the judgement was made.

Confidence and likelihood answer different questions:

- **Likelihood:** how probable the assessed event or explanation is considered to be.
- **Confidence:** how strong the information and reasoning supporting that assessment are.

A plausible assessment can therefore have low confidence when evidence is sparse.
